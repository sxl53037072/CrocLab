In many job markets — especially across Asia, Europe, the Middle East, and Latin America — a resume without a photo is an incomplete resume. Recruiters in Germany, China, Japan, South Korea, and dozens of other countries expect a professional headshot attached to your CV. Even in the U.S. and UK, where photos on resumes are less common, a polished headshot for LinkedIn, company directories, and conference badges is increasingly expected.

The problem: studios charge $50–$200 for a session. You get 3–5 photos. If you change jobs twice in three years, that's $100–$400 spent on headshots alone.

The alternative: your phone, a window, and 15 minutes. Done right, the result is indistinguishable from a studio shot — and free.

## What makes a resume photo "professional"

Before shooting, know the target. Resume photos across regions share these standards:

| Element | Standard |
|---|---|
| Framing | Head and upper shoulders; face fills ~60–70% of frame |
| Expression | Neutral to slight smile; approachable but composed |
| Background | Plain white, light gray, or soft solid color |
| Lighting | Even, no harsh shadows; skin tones natural |
| Eye contact | Looking directly at the lens |
| Attire | Industry-appropriate (blazer for corporate, clean casual for tech) |
| Resolution | ≥600×600 px; ideally 800×800+ |

Note the differences from a passport photo: resume photos allow slight smiles, don't require specific head-size ratios, and can use colored backgrounds. They're less strict — but still demand clean, professional presentation.

## The 15-minute home setup

### Step 1: Find your light

Light quality determines 80% of whether a photo looks professional or amateur. You need **soft, directional light** — and the best free source is a window.

- Face the window directly, 2–3 feet away
- Best conditions: overcast day, or morning/late afternoon diffused light
- Avoid direct midday sun — it creates hard shadows under the nose and chin
- Never rely on overhead room lights alone — they create unflattering under-eye shadows

If the sun is harsh, tape a white bedsheet over the window as a diffuser. This mimics the $300 softboxes studios use.

### Step 2: Set up the background

Three options, ranked by ease:

1. **Plain white or light gray wall** — Stand 3–4 feet in front of it (this creates natural separation and avoids your shadow hitting the wall).
2. **Hung fabric** — A solid-colored bedsheet pinned behind you. White, light gray, or light blue are universally safe.
3. **Any background + digital removal** — Shoot anywhere, then swap the background digitally. This is the most flexible approach — more on this below.

Avoid: bookshelves, kitchen counters, messy rooms, glass reflections, or anything that says "I took this in my apartment at 11 PM."

### Step 3: Camera position and framing

- **Height:** Camera at your eye level. Prop your phone on books, use a tripod, or have someone hold it.
- **Distance:** 4–6 feet (1.2–1.8 m). This avoids the wide-angle distortion that makes close-range selfies unflattering.
- **Use portrait mode** if your phone has it — the background blur mimics a studio lens.
- **Crop:** Head and shoulders. Leave slight space above your head.
- **Orientation:** Vertical or square. Most resume formats and profile systems use portrait orientation.

> **Pro tip:** Use your phone's 2× zoom (telephoto lens) instead of the wide-angle default. This compresses facial features naturally — exactly what portrait photographers do with 85mm lenses. The difference is subtle but immediately makes the photo look more professional.

### Step 4: Posture and expression

- **Angle your shoulders** 10–15° from the camera (not square-on). This adds dimension.
- **Slight smile** — Think "I just heard something mildly pleasant." Not a grin, not a forced smile. Relaxed.
- **Eyes directly at the lens** — not the screen, the tiny camera hole.
- **Chin slightly forward and down** — This defines your jawline and eliminates any double-chin effect.
- **Take 30+ photos.** Burst mode. Micro-expressions change frame by frame; only 1 in 15 will be the one.

### Step 5: Pick and post-process

From your 30+ shots, pick the one where:
- Both eyes are equally open and looking at the lens
- Your expression is natural (not stiff, not over-smiling)
- Lighting is even across the face
- No motion blur

Then you need three edits:
1. **Background cleanup or replacement** — Even a white wall usually isn't perfectly white
2. **Crop to the right aspect ratio** — Square, 3:4, or your resume template's spec
3. **Minor brightness/contrast** — Your phone's built-in editor handles this

## Resume photo specs by region

Different regions have different conventions:

| Region | Photo expected on resume? | Typical size | Background | Notes |

## FAQ

**Q: Should I put a photo on my resume if I'm applying in the U.S. or UK?**
A: For the resume itself — typically no, as some companies prefer photo-free resumes to reduce bias. However, you still need a professional headshot for LinkedIn, and many companies request one during onboarding. Having a polished photo ready is never a disadvantage.

**Q: What color background should I use for a Chinese resume photo?**
A: Blue is the most common for Chinese resumes (简历照). White is also accepted. Red backgrounds are typically reserved for official government documents. IDSnap offers all three as one-tap options.

**Q: Can I use a selfie for my resume?**
A: Not directly — the wide-angle distortion of a front-facing camera at arm's length makes features look disproportionate. But if you use the rear camera with a timer at 4+ feet, or have someone else take the photo, the result can be fully professional.

**Q: Do I need to wear a suit?**
A: Match your industry. Finance, law, and consulting: yes. Tech, creative, and startups: a clean, solid-colored top is fine. Healthcare and academia: business casual. When in doubt, a collared shirt or blazer is universally safe. If you don't have formal wear handy, IDSnap's outfit overlay can add one digitally.

**Q: How is a resume photo different from a passport photo?**
A: Resume photos are less strict — slight smiles are fine, background colors can vary, and you have more flexibility with framing and expression. Passport photos require neutral expression, specific head-size ratios, and usually a white background. The lighting and at-home setup are essentially the same for both.
